I Had the Right Plan. I Ran Half of It.

October 2026

I hadn't interviewed in four years. I'm a platform engineer, I'm good at the job, and it still took four months and forty rejections to find the next one.

What follows is what those months actually looked like, and what I got wrong. The second part is longer.

The numbers

Four months of searching produced:

  • 4 take-home assignments
  • 5 technical interviews
  • 2 system design rounds
  • 40 rejections
  • 1 offer

Eleven rounds that asked me to actually build or design something. Forty rejections. One job.

Nobody posts the rejection count. It's the one I wanted to see when I was thinking something was wrong with me.

What changed between month one and month four

I made a Trello board and started drilling fundamentals: Python, data structures and algorithms, system design. The board came from Esco Obong's study plan: topics to learn, topics in progress, topics finished. I got better.

Then I'd sit down in an interview and blank on things I knew. I wasn't concise. I got flustered.

What I couldn't prepare for on my own was the clock. So I hired a tutor, and the whole job was to sit there and time me while I solved problems live in a plain terminal IDE. No autocorrect. No suggestions. No AI. As if I was interviewing.

Under that pressure the cracks showed up fast: where I couldn't say things out loud, and where the fundamentals I thought I had weren't there. We kept smoothing them over until I could handle most flavors of problem without coming apart.

After that I had AI generate variations of the same problems, easy through hard, and set the bar at finishing with fifteen minutes to spare. AI wrote the problems. It just wasn't in the room when I solved them.

The plan I had been following has two phases. Learn the concepts without a clock, then practice against one. I ran the first phase for months and walked into real interviews having never run the second. It also says to do three mock interviews before you interview for real. I did my first mock after I had already failed several loops.

I had the right plan. I ran half of it, in the wrong order.

The loop that went worst

All of them went badly. Each one taught me something, which is the generous way of saying none of them went well.

One was worse than the rest by a distance. It was a CodeSignal assessment. I had practiced the main CodeSignal problems on my own and was generally reaching level 4 by the end of my time. Once there was a real timer on it, I couldn't get out of level 2.

So I drilled the foundations harder, and in desperation I started memorizing, hoping it would balance the scales. The plan says, in as many words, not to do that. Understand why it's built the way it is, don't memorize the code. I did it anyway, because I was out of time. On the day of the assessment, that was a disaster.

I had to practice timed.

Using it every day is not knowing it

At work you're never in a position where you can't ask for help, look something up, ask AI, or just take your time and think it through. You're certainly never doing it with a stranger watching your screen while you are, in effect, signing for your dinner.

The questions are there to probe how well you know something. How well you know it, not how well you can look it up or prompt for it.

It wasn't that I didn't know anything. It was that the level that got the job done was not the level needed to convince a company to pay you six figures to solve their problem.

I had written Kubernetes manifests and set custom Helm values for years. Then someone asked me how I would secure the Kubernetes API server. I had been working the surface of a tool I had never looked inside.

What forty rejections does to you

It makes you want to give up.

I asked myself, seriously, whether I should find another profession.

What I'd tell myself in month one

Those four years away from interviewing cost me more than I knew. The whole layout had changed and I walked in blind to it.

I was interviewing the way I interviewed as a junior, back when the industry rewarded aptitude. Knowing how to get to the answer counted for a lot. The requirements were Linux, git, some CI/CD, some API exposure, and willing to learn.

That's not the bar anymore. Now it's cloud, Kubernetes, GitOps, security, and infrastructure, and you're expected to know them from the foundation up.

Willing to learn got me in the door for years. It is not what gets you in the door now.

You have to grow. You have to do the uncomfortable thing and get in front of your peers and other engineers and practice interviewing out loud. There is no way around it. Start now.

And do not stop interviewing. Even when you are not looking. It is the only way to find out what the market is asking for and which skills it expects you to already have.
